iMAP

A multi-mode campus navigation app for UIUC — designed so that every student, regardless of ability, language, or schedule, can find what they need and get there without friction.

Type Course project, IS 333
Timeline 2026
Role UX Designer, team project

Overview

One campus, many different needs

Campus maps are designed for the average user. But the average user doesn't exist — UIUC has students with mobility needs, international students navigating in a second language, athletes looking for courts that aren't already packed, and commuters trying to find parking between classes.

iMAP is a multi-mode campus navigation app that lets users switch context based on what they actually need right now — not what the map assumes they need by default. 16 screens, 6 modes, one consistent design system.

The Problem

Campus tools are built for one person

The existing UIUC map and navigation tools share a common flaw: they serve the majority use case and leave everyone else to piece information together from scattered sources. Three specific frustrations drove the design:

01
You walk across campus and the space is full

After a late class or a delayed lunch, a student might cross campus to the library or gym — only to find every seat taken or the court occupied. There's no way to check before leaving.

02
Accessibility information is buried or missing

Students with mobility needs have to manually verify elevator locations and accessible entrances — information that should be the default, not a special case.

03
International students navigate in their second language

Campus tools rarely offer meaningful language support. For students who are still building English fluency, this adds friction to every interaction.

Core Value

Inclusivity — the belief that a campus map should work equally well for every person, regardless of ability, language, schedule, or lifestyle.

Target Users

Six types of people, one app

Rather than designing for a single persona, iMAP was built around six distinct user contexts — each of which maps to a specific mode in the app.

Students with mobility needs

Need accessible routes, elevator locations, and slope-friendly paths — as a primary feature, not an afterthought.

International students

Need interface language switching so navigation doesn't require fluency in English to use effectively.

Students between classes

Need to know where to study for the next 45 minutes — which libraries have open seats right now.

Athletes and fitness users

Need real-time occupancy for courts and gyms before making the trip across campus.

Commuters

Need parking, bike racks, and scooter areas mapped alongside class locations.

Arts and culture seekers

Need campus events, exhibitions, and performances surfaced in context — not buried in a separate calendar.

Six Modes

Switch the mode, change what you see

The core interaction is mode-switching. Each mode filters the map and surfaces only the information relevant to the user's current context. The same campus looks different depending on what you need from it.

♿
Accessibility

Elevator locations, accessible entrances, slope-friendly routes. Treated as primary, not secondary.

📚
Study Spaces

Libraries and quiet areas with real-time occupancy so you know before you walk over.

🏀

🍜

🅿️
Mobility and Parking

Bike racks, scooter areas, and car parking with real-time availability.

🎭
Arts and Culture

Performances, exhibitions, and events surfaced in the map view.

Design Decisions

What shaped the final design

01
Dark mode as default

The app is used outdoors, often in direct sunlight or low-light evening conditions. Dark backgrounds with high-contrast orange accents perform better in both contexts than a light theme.

02
AI chatbot for natural language queries

Rather than requiring users to know the correct mode and filters upfront, the AI chatbot (Masey) lets users ask in plain language: "Where can I study near my next class?" It then translates intent into mode + location.

03
Day route from class schedule

Users upload their class timetable and the app plans the whole day — suggesting study spots and dining options between classes so dead time becomes intentional time.

04
Weather-triggered mode switching

When rain is detected, the app surfaces a warning and automatically adjusts route suggestions to favor covered paths — a small detail that reflects real campus navigation behavior.

05
Language switching built into onboarding

Language preference is set during the first-run experience — English, Chinese, Spanish, French, and Japanese — so international students are never using a tool that assumes English fluency.

Onboarding — year, language, accessibility settings, interests (S1–S5)
Core screens — home map, location detail, AI chatbot Mappy, schedule upload, day route (S6–S10)
Mode screens — study spaces, exercise, dining sorted by wait time, weather warning (S11–S14)
Profile, full Chinese interface, and arrival confirmation screen (S15–S16)
Reflection

What I'd push further

Real-time occupancy data was central to the value proposition but remains a design assumption. The next step would be working with UIUC Facilities to understand what sensor data actually exists and what would need to be built.
The AI chatbot interface (Masey) was designed but not deeply tested. Conversational UI for navigation is genuinely hard — edge cases around ambiguous queries and incorrect intent mapping need structured usability testing to get right.
Designing six modes meant constant tension between consistency and specificity. The design system held together visually, but each mode had slightly different information hierarchies — something a real engineering handoff would need to resolve more explicitly.
This project reinforced that accessibility design works best when it's treated as a primary constraint from day one, not a feature added at the end. Every routing decision looked different once we asked "does this work for someone in a wheelchair?" first.
