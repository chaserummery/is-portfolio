MailMind

An AI-powered email organizer for college students. Surfaces deadlines, emergency alerts, and package arrivals automatically — so the inbox stops feeling like a to-do list you have to decode.

Type Course project, IS 226 HCI
Timeline Spring 2026, 10 weeks
Role Co-designer (with Cathy)
Tools Figma · React · TypeScript · Cursor
Overview

The inbox was broken for students

College students receive dozens of emails daily from professors, university offices, student organizations — mixed in with ads, scam alerts, and low-priority notifications. Standard email clients treat a campus emergency and a dining hall newsletter with the same visual weight.

MailMind is a mobile email organizer that automatically categorizes incoming emails, surfaces urgent information, and generates AI summaries per email. The project went through three design iterations and was presented at an iSchool Lightning Talk in May 2026.

The Challenge

Three problems, one broken inbox

Through informal interviews with UIUC classmates and professors, we identified the core pain points:

01
Too much noise

Important emails from professors get buried under ads, promotions, and low-priority notifications. Students miss deadlines not because they forgot, but because the email never stood out.

02
No urgency signal

A campus emergency alert and a dining hall newsletter look identical in a standard inbox. There's no way to know at a glance what actually needs attention right now.

03
Cognitive overload

Long emails from professors require students to read everything just to find one sentence — a due date, a room number, a deadline. The information is there; finding it isn't.

04
Existing tools don't fit

Superhuman and Fyxer AI are priced above $20/month and designed for working professionals — not students managing academic life on a limited budget.

Research

Talking to real students

We interviewed UIUC classmates and professors with two core questions: what types of emails do you most frequently miss, and what information do you wish your inbox surfaced automatically?

Professors noted that students consistently miss deadline reminders even when they were emailed multiple times.

Nearly all students mentioned ads and promotions as the primary source of inbox clutter.

Missed package pickup notifications were a recurring frustration — packages sat at front desks for days.

The most-requested feature across interviews was automatic deadline extraction from professor emails.

After seeing early prototypes, AI email summary and to-do aggregation were consistently rated most useful.

Design Process

Three versions, one clear direction

The project moved through three distinct iterations, each answering a different question.

V1
Hand-drawn sketches — What should exist?

Mapped out core feature set and information architecture on paper. The key question: what categories of information do students actually need surfaced? We landed on five: Assignments, Appointments, Packages, Events, and Notifications.

V2
Low-fidelity wireframes — How should each module look?

Focused on layout and module structure. Tested how much information each card should show at a glance, where the AI summary should sit relative to the email body, and how emergency alerts should interrupt normal inbox flow without becoming annoying.

V3
High-fidelity Figma prototype — Does it actually work?

Fully designed, click-through prototype with 6 screens: Login, Inbox, Email Detail, Alert Detail, Tool dashboard, and Profile. Built to be fully navigable by button tap for live demonstration at the Lightning Talk.

Solution

Six screens, every edge case considered

Key design decisions that shaped the final prototype:

Pain points mapped to specific features
AI-summarized dashboard with deadlines, appointments, packages
Final six screens: Login, Inbox, Email, Alert Detail, Tool, Profile
01
Color-coded labels

Teal for Assignment, orange for Package, blue for Notification, purple for Appointment. Allows instant visual scanning without reading every subject line.

02
Persistent emergency alert banner

Appears above the inbox and cannot be scrolled past. Getting the visual weight right — urgent enough to interrupt, not so alarming users dismiss it immediately — required the most iteration of any single component.

03
AI Summary card

Embedded at the top of each email, showing the actionable information extracted from the body. Students see what they need to do before reading the full email.

04
Tool screen as central dashboard

Aggregates all deadlines, appointments, package arrivals, and events across linked accounts in one view. The feature that tested highest with users.

Outcome

Lightning Talk, May 2026

Presented at the UIUC iSchool Lightning Talk to students, faculty, and design peers. Audience feedback validated the AI summary feature as the highest-value addition — particularly for long emails from professors and university administration.

A partial implementation was built using Cursor and React. The front-end UI was functional, but Google OAuth verification was not completed within the project timeline — which became its own lesson in scoping.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

Complete Google OAuth integration first. Without real email data, the AI categorization logic was never tested against actual inbox content — which means we were always designing for a hypothetical, not a real one.
Run structured usability tests rather than conversational interviews. We knew users liked the concept; we didn't know whether they could navigate the Tool screen without guidance.
Design the desktop version in parallel. Most students check email on laptop, not phone. The mobile-first decision made sense for scoping, but it shaped features in ways that wouldn't translate well.
The emergency alert interaction taught me the most. Hierarchy in UI isn't just about size — it's about timing, interruptibility, and what you make someone do before they can dismiss something.
