OrangeNest 橙舍

A sublease platform built for UIUC international students who need housing fast. Real listings, direct WeChat contact, filters for the details that actually matter — including pets.

Type Personal project, self-initiated
Timeline 2026, ongoing
Role Sole designer and developer
Stack Figma · HTML/CSS · Supabase · Vercel
Origin

It started with a real emergency

A friend needed short-term housing near UIUC on short notice. I spent hours making calls, adding strangers on WeChat, and refreshing Xiaohongshu — operating at 1pm US time while every contact in China was still asleep.

"We eventually found a place because someone happened to see our wanted post and reached out. Luck is not a product."

The problem wasn't a lack of housing or a lack of people looking to sublease. It was that both sides had no reliable way to find each other quickly. That gap is what OrangeNest was built to close.

The Problem

Six problems, one impossible search

The housing search surfaced six distinct pain points, all layered on top of each other:

01
Time zone friction

Searching at 1–2pm US time means agents and contacts in China aren't active yet. For a last-minute need, a few hours of waiting is the difference between finding housing and not.

02
Unreliable intermediaries

"I'll check for you." "Add me on WeChat." "Leave your number." Every agent had a version of this. None of them followed through. The problem wasn't the absence of agents — it was having no way to know if any of them would actually deliver.

03
Scattered information

Xiaohongshu required constant searching, posting wanted listings, and manually filtering through posts with no indication of availability. Finding a listing meant another round of WeChat messages before anything was confirmed.

04
Short-term supply is thin

Most platforms carry long-term leases. Agents confirmed this directly: for stays around a month, sublease is the only realistic option. The need wasn't "find an apartment" — it was "find someone who's leaving and willing to hand over their lease temporarily."

05
Pet restrictions narrow the pool further

My friend has a cat. A listing that looked perfect on every other dimension could still fall through at the last step. This filter needed to be surfaced upfront, not buried in a conversation.

06
No single source of truth

The core problem wasn't any one of the above. It was that there was nowhere to see which subleases were actually available right now, with move-in date, end date, price, pet policy, and direct contact visible up front.

Core Insight

This wasn't a housing problem. It was a last-minute matching problem. Supply and demand existed simultaneously — they just had no shared, reliable place to meet.

Design Decisions

Every choice came from the search itself

I didn't run formal user interviews for this project. The research was my own experience — and being the person with the need made the product requirements unusually clear. I knew exactly what information I needed before deciding to contact someone.

Brand
Sunset orange from Champaign itself

Orange is UIUC's primary school color. For students searching for housing near campus, the connection is immediate. The palette pulls warm, residential, not corporate — closer to high-end real estate than a generic rental app.

Contact
WeChat-first, no middleman

This is how the UIUC Chinese student community already communicates. Adding another messaging layer would have recreated the exact friction the platform was built to remove.

Listing card
Six things before you decide to contact

Address, price, available from and to, occupancy dates, pet policy, urgency flag. Everything a searcher needs to decide whether it's worth reaching out — visible without clicking through.

Publish flow
Three steps, low commitment

Most sublease platforms ask for too much upfront. Three steps keeps the barrier low enough that someone with a lease to hand off will actually post — not abandon the form halfway.

Early Validation

Someone recommended it to me

What happened

After posting a demo video on Xiaohongshu, a student I had previously contacted during my own housing search reached out — not knowing I had built it. He forwarded my video to me and said he thought it looked useful, that it was made by a UIUC student, and that I should check it out if I ever needed housing again.

He had a unit available but was back in China at the time, trying to find a sublease quickly. We had spoken earlier but couldn't agree on price and move-in dates. He didn't know he was recommending my own product to me.

That moment confirmed two things: the problem is real enough that people recognize the solution when they see it, and the gap between supply and demand exists on both sides simultaneously.

Technical Build

Learned the stack by building it

I used this project as the context for learning Supabase — setting up the listings table, row-level security policies, and a public storage bucket for listing photos. The front-end is HTML and CSS. Deployed on Vercel.

The stack was chosen to minimize cost and maximize iteration speed. The entire platform runs for free at current scale. A Figma prototype of a housing platform is an argument. A deployed platform with a working database is evidence.

Reflection

What comes next

A WeChat mini-program version. That's where the actual target users already spend most of their time. A web platform solves discovery; a mini-program would solve the whole workflow.
A lightweight availability verification layer. The absence of this is exactly what made Xiaohongshu frustrating — listings with no indication of whether they're still available. Even a simple "last confirmed available" timestamp would help.
The biggest constraint I self-imposed was learning the full stack while building. It slowed the timeline, but it meant every part of the product was something I understood completely — not a black box I'd designed around.
